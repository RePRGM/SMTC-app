How to count music for dance

"5, 6, 7, 8!" and you are already lost. Counting music looks like a secret language, but it comes down to a handful of simple ideas. Learn them once and they work for every dance you will ever do.

By Show Me The Counts · Updated June 2026 · 8 min read

If you have ever frozen at the edge of a dance floor, unsure when to start, you have run into the one skill underneath all dancing: counting the music. The good news is that "having rhythm" is not something you are born with or without. It is a skill, and like any skill it breaks down into steps you can practice.

This guide walks through those steps in order: find the pulse, count it in eights, find the 1, hear the beats in between, and feel the bigger phrase. Master these and you will stop guessing and start hearing the structure that is sitting right there in almost every song.

step 1Find the pulse

Before any numbers, there is just the pulse: the steady beat you would tap your foot to without thinking. Every other layer of counting is built on top of it, so this is where you start.

The trick is to listen past the singing. Lyrics and melody sit on the surface and pull your attention around, but the pulse lives underneath in the drums and bass. Think of the vocals as a television playing in the room: to hear the quieter, steadier sounds, you mentally turn the TV off. Pick one steady instrument, usually the kick drum or a repeating bass note, and follow that single thread.

The pulse is the even, unbroken tick under everything. Clap along until you can hold it through a whole verse.

Test yourself: tap your finger on that beat and keep it steady through the verse and the chorus. If you can hold an even tap without rushing or dragging, you have found the pulse. That is the foundation. Everything below is just learning to organize it.

Tune out the lyrics, follow one steady instrument, and tap until it holds.

step 2Count it in eights

Now put numbers to that pulse. Dancers count to eight: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, then start over. Musicians usually count to four, so why the difference? Because a measure of most dance music is four beats, but a complete musical phrase, one full musical thought, usually lasts two measures. Counting to eight lines your steps up with that whole idea instead of half of it.

One eight-count equals two bars of four. The 1 opens the phrase; the 5 opens its second half.

This eight-count is the skeleton that choreographers and teachers use to map out moves, which is why class instructions sound like "and on the 3 you turn, on the 5 and 6 you travel." Once you can count steady eights along with a song, you can place any step exactly where it belongs. Almost all dance music shares this structure. The main exception is waltz, which moves in threes and gets counted in sixes.

A measure is four. A phrase is eight. Dancers count the phrase.

step 3Find the 1

Counting eights only works if you start them in the right place, and that means finding the 1: the first and strongest beat of the phrase, the downbeat where everything resets. Most steps in most dances begin here, so this is the beat worth hunting for.

The 1 announces itself with emphasis. Listen for something a little heavier or brighter: a deeper drum hit, a cymbal, a chord change, or the first word of a new sung line. It also just feels like a beginning, the way the first word of a sentence does. There is a quieter cousin too: the 5, the downbeat that opens the second half of the phrase. The 1 and the 5 feel similar, but the 1 carries a touch more weight.

Both are downbeats, but the 1 glows brightest. When in doubt, the beat that feels like "home" is the 1.

This is also why teachers count you in with "5, 6, 7, 8." They are not starting on the 5 for any deep reason. It is a shared countdown so the whole room lands on the next 1 together. Different instruments point to the 1 in different ways depending on the style of music, which is where the genre-specific guides below come in.

The 1 is the strongest beat and the start of the phrase. Find it, and the count falls into place.

step 4Hear the beats in between

So far every count has landed on a whole beat. But faster footwork and syncopation live in the spaces between them. Split each beat in half and you get the and-counts: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and." The "and" is the exact midpoint between two numbers.

One measure, split in half: the numbers are the beats, the & sits exactly between them.

You can split things even finer into quarter-counts, usually spoken "1 e and a," but you rarely need that as a beginner. For now, just knowing the "and" exists unlocks a lot, because plenty of steps and styling hits happen off the beat rather than on it. When a move feels like it lands "in the cracks," it is almost always on an "and."

Whole beats are the numbers. The half beats between them are the "ands."

step 5Feel the bigger phrase

Zoom out one more level. Just as beats group into eights, eights group into larger phrases, very often four eight-counts that add up to a 32-beat block. This is the unit a verse or a chorus tends to ride on, and the music usually signals a small "arrival" at the end of one before the next begins.

Four eights often make one big phrase. The fourth tends to resolve, setting up a fresh 1.

You do not need to count this consciously to use it. As your ear develops, you will start to feel where a section lifts, builds, and lands, and you will know a new phrase is coming before it arrives. That sense of "what happens next" is the difference between dancing on top of the music and dancing with it. Not every song is a tidy 32 beats, but the feeling of phrases gathering and resolving is nearly universal.

Beats build eights. Eights build phrases. Phrases are where musicality lives.

train your earThe fastest way to learn this: isolate the instruments

Every step above gets dramatically easier when you can hear the rhythm section on its own. In a full song the drums and bass are buried under vocals, melody, and harmony, which is exactly what makes the beat hard to find in the first place. So pull them out.

practiceA simple routine to build the skill

Counting music is learned off the floor as much as on it. A few minutes a day goes a long way: pick a song with a strong, obvious beat and clap the pulse all the way through. Then count it in eights out loud, over and over, until you can find the 1 without hunting. When that feels easy, move to a song with a softer beat and do it again. Slow tracks down when they get away from you, loop the parts that trip you up, and keep the count running in your head even when you are just listening on the train. The structure stops being something you chase and becomes something you simply hear.

Frequently asked questions

What is an 8-count in dance?

An 8-count is two measures of 4/4 music counted as one unit: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. It is the basic building block dancers use to map steps to music, because a single musical phrase usually spans those eight beats.

Why do dancers count to 8 instead of 4?

Musicians usually count in 4, which is one measure. But a complete musical phrase normally lasts two measures, so dancers count to 8 to line up with that longer musical idea and with the way choreography is structured.

What is the 1 in music?

The 1 is the first and strongest beat of the eight-count, the downbeat where a musical phrase begins. It is usually marked by an accent such as a heavier drum hit or the start of a new melodic line, and most dance steps start on it.

Why do dance teachers count "5, 6, 7, 8"?

Teachers count "5, 6, 7, 8" as a lead-in so the whole class starts moving on the next 1 together. It is a shared countdown into the top of the phrase, not a different timing.

I have no rhythm. Can I still learn to count music?

Almost anyone can learn to count music. It is a trainable skill, not a talent you are born with. Start with a song that has a strong, clear beat, tap along with the pulse, then count it in sets of eight. With practice the structure becomes obvious.
