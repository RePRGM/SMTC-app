The best apps to slow down and practice music for dancing

Dancers reach for these apps to do a handful of specific things: slow a song to learn footwork, loop a hard section, isolate the beat to find the 1, and drill timing. Most apps nail one of those. Here is an honest look at the options, including where we fit and where we do not.

Three jobs, three kinds of app. The question for dancers is how many you need in one place.

By Show Me The Counts · Updated June 2026 · 9 min read

There is no single best app, only the best app for what you are trying to do. The market splits into three buckets: slow-down and loop tools for general practice, AI stem separators that pull a song apart, and rhythm trainers that teach timing. A couple of apps are built specifically for dancers, and the rest are aimed at musicians but work fine for dance. Below is the full comparison, then a short profile of each, then a plain recommendation by need.

The apps, profiled

The dancer all-in-one

Show Me The Counts

SMTC is the one built around dancers rather than musicians, and its pitch is breadth in one place: slow a song without changing pitch, loop a section, isolate the instruments to find the beat, and break a track into Moments with reference videos for learning choreography. It adds things no general tool has, like a salsa Rhythm Machine for drilling timing and a compilation shuffle that plays your saved Moments in random order to train adaptation.

Where it does not lead: its instrument isolation is built for dance practice, not the surgical, every-instrument stem editing Moises offers, and its rhythm generator currently focuses on salsa. If you want one dance-first app for the whole practice loop, this is the gap it fills.

The stem-separation heavyweightMoises

If your main need is pulling a song apart, Moises is the leader. Its AI separates vocals, drums, bass, and more with quality the others here cannot match, and it layers on pitch and tempo change, chord and key detection, a metronome, count-in, and part looping, across iOS, Android, web, and desktop. The free tier limits how many songs you can separate each month, with paid plans starting around $3.99 per month.

The catch for dancers is that Moises is a musician's tool. There is no choreography workflow, no reference video per section, no dance rhythm generator, and separation needs an internet connection. For isolating instruments to study a song, it is excellent. As your one dance-practice app, it leaves gaps.

Best raw stem separation here. Built for musicians, not dancers.

The free salsa rhythm trainerSalsa Beat Machine

A beloved free tool, the Salsa Beat Machine generates salsa (and merengue) rhythms you can take apart instrument by instrument, with a voice counting On-1 and On-2, tempo and key control, and both 2/3 and 3/2 clave. For a beginner trying to hear the clave, the cowbell, and the 1, it is one of the best free resources that exists, and the web version costs nothing.

Its limit is scope. It plays generated rhythms, not your own songs, so you cannot slow down, loop, or isolate instruments inside the actual tracks you dance to, and it is salsa only. Think of it as a focused ear-training instrument rather than a full practice app.

Free and excellent for salsa timing. Synthetic rhythms only.

The musician slow-downersAnytune & Amazing Slow Downer

These are the long-standing, rock-solid tools for slowing music down and looping sections without wrecking the pitch. Anytune adds marks, loops, pitch shift, and a count-in with a polished interface, and Amazing Slow Downer has been the reliable classic for decades. Both do basic instrument reduction through EQ tricks rather than true AI stems.

They are aimed at musicians learning solos, so they have no dance-specific features, but if all you want is dependable slow-and-loop, they do it beautifully. Anytune leans iOS and Mac with a free tier plus a Pro upgrade; Amazing Slow Downer is a paid app across platforms.

The dancer-made slow/loop toolsBeatLoop & TuneTempo

Both are built by and for dancers. BeatLoop focuses on frame-accurate looping, slowing without pitch change, and recording a practice video synced to your loop, with free and paid tiers (around €2.99 per month). TuneTempo is a free, ad-free web tool from Salsa Vida that slows, loops, and exports a slowed track, ideal when you just want to drop a song into the browser and drill a section.

Neither isolates instruments or generates rhythms, so they are lighter than SMTC or Moises, but for clean, dance-minded slow-and-loop they are a great, low-friction choice.

The budget and general optionsTempo SlowMo & Music Speed Changer

Cheaper, more general tools round out the field. Tempo SlowMo slows and speeds audio without pitch change, loops, and drops markers, with a BPM tapper and EQ as in-app purchases. Music Speed Changer is a free web and mobile tool that changes speed and pitch independently, loops, and saves an MP3. Both get the basic job done; neither is built for dance, and the experience is plainer than the dancer-focused options.

Which should you pick?

You want one app for the whole dance practice loop. Show Me The Counts. Slowing, looping, instrument isolation, choreography sections, and timing drills in one dance-first place.
You mainly need to isolate instruments or remove vocals. Moises, for the best stem separation, especially if you also play an instrument.
You are learning to hear the 1 in salsa, for free. The Salsa Beat Machine, then move to a full app once your ear is trained.
You just want reliable slow-and-loop. Anytune or Amazing Slow Downer for musicians, BeatLoop or TuneTempo if you want a dance-made tool.
You want free and simple right now. TuneTempo or Music Speed Changer in the browser, or the free tiers of SMTC, Moises, and BeatLoop.

Our honest take

If you only do one of these things, a specialist will usually do it slightly better: Moises separates stems more powerfully, the Salsa Beat Machine drills salsa timing for free, and Anytune is a more mature slow-downer. SMTC's case is not that it beats each specialist at its one job. It is that dancers rarely need only one job, and stitching three apps together is its own kind of friction. SMTC brings the slow-down, the loop, the instrument isolation, the rhythm drills, and the choreography tools into a single app designed for how dancers actually practice. That is the trade, and for most dancers it is the right one.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best app to slow down music for dancing?

It depends on what you need. For an all-in-one dancer tool that slows, loops, isolates instruments, and helps with choreography, Show Me The Counts is built for that. For the most powerful instrument isolation, Moises leads. For free salsa timing practice, the Salsa Beat Machine is excellent. For musician-grade slow-down and looping, Anytune and Amazing Slow Downer are long-standing favorites.

What is the best app to isolate instruments or remove vocals from a song?

Moises has the most powerful and flexible AI stem separation among consumer apps, isolating vocals, drums, bass, and more. SMTC's Layers also isolates instruments, but inside a dance-practice workflow with looping, slowing, and sections rather than as a standalone stem editor.

Is there a free app for slowing down or practicing music for dance?

Yes. The Salsa Beat Machine (web, salsa rhythms), TuneTempo (web slow-down and loop), and Music Speed Changer (web) are free, and SMTC, Moises, Anytune, and BeatLoop all offer free tiers with paid upgrades for advanced features.

What is a good Moises alternative for dancers?

Show Me The Counts is the closest dancer-focused alternative. Moises is built around musicians and stem editing, while SMTC wraps instrument isolation in dance-specific tools: looping on phrase boundaries, slowing down, marking sections with reference videos, a salsa rhythm generator, and a shuffle mode for practicing adaptation.

Can these apps slow a song down without changing the pitch?

Most of the good ones do, including SMTC, Moises, Anytune, Amazing Slow Downer, BeatLoop, TuneTempo, and Tempo SlowMo. Avoid tools that simply change playback speed, since those drop the pitch and distort the audio, which makes the music harder to follow.
