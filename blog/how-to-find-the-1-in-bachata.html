How to find the 1 in bachata using the instruments

Bachata hands you two gifts salsa never does: a scraper that never stops ticking, and a bass that plants itself right on the downbeat. Learn to hear them and the 1 finds you.

Bachata moves in eight-count phrases. You step on 1 and 5, and tap on 4 and 8.

By Show Me The Counts · Updated June 2026 · 7 min read

You hear "1, 2, 3, tap" and try to count along, but the song keeps slipping out from under you. The tap comes too early, or too late, or you realize halfway through a song that you started on the wrong foot entirely. Counting harder does not fix it.

Here is the secret that turns it around: finding the 1 is a listening skill, not a counting skill. The band is not counting numbers, and neither should you. Each instrument in a bachata group has a job, and a couple of them are quietly telling you exactly where the downbeat is. Learn their patterns and the 1 stops being a guess.

A quick map first. Bachata is 4/4 music counted in eight-beat phrases. You step on 1, 2, 3 and tap on 4, then step on 5, 6, 7 and tap on 8. The two downbeats, where your basic begins, are the 1 and the 5. The taps, that little hip motion or toe touch, land on 4 and 8. As in salsa, the 1 and the 5 feel almost identical, so plenty of "I can't find the 1" is really "I can't tell the 1 from the 5." Do not worry about that yet. First, just land on a downbeat so you are on time. We will separate the 1 from the 5 at the end.

The good news: bachata is one of the friendlier Latin genres for this, because two instruments do most of the work for you. Let's go through them, easiest first.

01 / your metronomeLock onto the guira first

The guira is the metal scraper that gives bachata its constant shimmering "chick-a-chick." It is the primary timekeeper of the whole band, and the key word is constant: in the basic derecho rhythm it scrapes every single eighth note, filling every gap, never pausing. That is exactly what makes it your lifeline.

Guira: a nonstop scrape across every beat. It does not single out the 1, but it keeps you glued to the pulse so you never drift.

Here is the honest catch: because the guira plays everything, it does not point at the 1 on its own. What it does is keep you perfectly on time. Lock onto that scrape and you will hold the beat through turns, dips, and footwork even when your brain loses count. Think of it as the floor under your feet. You still need another instrument to tell you which beat is the 1, and that is where the bass comes in.

The guira keeps you on time. It is your anchor, not your compass.

02 / easiest 1Let the bass plant the downbeat

If the guira is the floor, the bass is the foundation underneath it, and it is the surest way to actually find the 1. Here is the part that matters most: in the basic derecho rhythm, the bass plays a long, low note right on the 1 and holds it for about a beat and a half. After that it pauses, slips in a short syncopated note, and fills in toward the next downbeat.

Bass: a long, low, sustained note lands square on the 1 (and the 5). On a loud floor it is often the clearest thing you can grab.

This is the big difference from salsa, where the bass deliberately dodges the 1. In bachata the bass does the opposite: it sits right down on the downbeat. That deep, sustained low note is your most reliable "this is the 1" signal in the whole band, and in a club it is often the one thing that cuts through the speakers. Whenever you feel lost, stop, listen for that low boom, and you have found your downbeat.

Hear the long, low note? That is the 1. Bachata's bass lands on it, not around it.

03 / the tapFeel the bongo's low hit on 4 and 8

The bongo is bachata's rhythmic heartbeat, and its basic pattern is called the martillo (Spanish for "hammer"). In the derecho it accents beats 1, 3, and 4, with crisp high hits on 1 and 3 and a heavier, lower hit on the 4 (and again on the 8). That low "boom" is the one to tune into.

Bongo: high hits on 1 and 3, then a heavy low drum on 4 and 8. That low boom is exactly where you tap, and it ramps you into the next downbeat.

This is wonderfully useful, because the bongo's low hit lands on the same 4 and 8 where your tap happens. Feel that boom and you have found the tap directly, and since the tap sits right before the next downbeat, the low hit also acts as a tiny ramp into the 1 and the 5. Listen for "boom, 1." The bongo has another job too: it throws in a fill to flag the song's section changes, which we will get to next.

Low boom on 4 and 8 is your tap, and the beat right after it is your downbeat.

04 / feelUse the guitars for phrasing, not timing

The requinto is the lead guitar, that crying, melodic voice that makes bachata sound like nothing else. The segunda (rhythm guitar) sits behind it, adding syncopated chordal texture on the offbeats. Neither one is a reliable timekeeper, so do not try to count to them.

What they are great for is phrasing and feeling. The requinto carries the melody and the emotion, and melodies tend to begin on the 1, so when a new guitar phrase or vocal line kicks off, that is a strong clue you are at the top of a phrase. Use the guitars to shape your styling and body movement, and to sense where the song breathes, while you keep your actual timing anchored to the guira, bass, and bongo.

Guitars tell you the story. Percussion and bass tell you the time.

structureKnow the section: derecho, majao, mambo

Bachata songs move through three rhythmic sections, and recognizing which one you are in tells you what to listen for and how to dance.

Derecho is the basic, smooth groove of the verses. The bongo is steady, the guira ticks along underneath, and your basics stay grounded and close. This is where everything above applies most cleanly. Majao is the chorus, where the energy lifts: the guira and bongo drive harder into the downbeats, the pulse hits stronger, and your dancing can open up. Mambo is the high-energy instrumental section where the band improvises and the singer usually drops out. It is the peak moment, made for footwork and shines.

How do you hear a new section coming? Listen for the bongo fill: a quick burst of notes the player drops in to mark the change. Almost every transition is announced by one, and because the new section lands on the 1, that fill is your heads-up that a fresh downbeat is about to arrive.

Each section has its own texture. Knowing where you are makes the 1 easier to predict.

drill itPractice by isolating the instruments

Everything above is happening at once in a real song, all five instruments layered together and fighting for your attention. To actually learn the guira's pulse or the bongo's low hit, you need to pull the rhythm section out of the mix and hear it on its own.

back to itTelling the 1 from the 5

Once you can reliably land on a downbeat, separating the true 1 from the 5 comes down to phrasing. Listen for the entrance of the bass after a break, the start of a sung line, or a new requinto melody, since these tend to begin on the 1. Section changes help too: when a song moves from derecho into majao, or the band drops into the mambo, that transition almost always resets on the 1. You do not need to force it. As your ear settles onto the downbeat, the larger phrase starts to make the 1 feel like home on its own.

Frequently asked questions

What does "the 1" mean in bachata?

The 1 is the first beat of the eight-count phrase bachata is danced to. You step on 1, 2, 3 and tap on 4, then step on 5, 6, 7 and tap on 8. The two downbeats are the 1 and the 5, and the taps land on 4 and 8. Finding the 1 means locating that first downbeat by ear so your basic lines up with the music.

Why is finding the 1 in bachata hard for beginners?

Beginners try to count numbers in their head instead of listening to the instruments. Bachata is actually one of the easier genres for this, because the guira gives you a nonstop pulse and the bass plants a long low note right on the 1. Once you know what to listen for, the music tells you where the downbeat is.

Which instrument helps you find the 1 in bachata?

Two of them. The guira is the constant scraping timekeeper that keeps you on beat and never stops, so it is your lifeline for staying on time. The bass is the surest marker of the downbeat itself: in the basic derecho rhythm it plays a long low note right on the 1. Lock onto the guira to stay on time, then use the bass to pin the 1.

What are the taps on 4 and 8 in bachata?

Bachata's basic step is step-step-step-tap. You step on 1, 2, 3 and tap (a hip motion or toe touch) on 4, then repeat on 5, 6, 7 with a tap on 8. In the music, the bongo's heavy low hit lands on the 4 and the 8, which is exactly where the tap happens.

What is the difference between derecho, majao, and mambo?

They are the three rhythmic sections of a bachata song. Derecho is the basic, smooth groove of the verses. Majao lifts the energy in the chorus, where the guira and bongo drive harder into the downbeats. Mambo is the high-energy instrumental section where the band improvises and dancers show footwork and shines. A bongo fill usually signals the move from one section to the next.
