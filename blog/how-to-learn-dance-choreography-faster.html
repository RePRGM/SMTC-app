How to learn dance choreography faster

You do not have a bad memory. You have a slow method. Stop swallowing the routine whole, break it into pieces, tie it to the music, and it sticks in a fraction of the time.

Break the routine into eight-count chunks. Master one, link it to the next, and the whole piece adds up.

By Show Me The Counts · Updated June 2026 · 8 min read

You nail the routine in class, walk out feeling great, and by the next morning half of it has evaporated. It is the most demoralizing part of dancing, and almost everyone blames their memory. The memory is fine. The method is the problem. Most people try to memorize choreography as one long list of steps, which is the slowest, most fragile way to do it. There is a much faster way, and it is mostly about structure and the music.

Here is the method, in the order you should use it: chunk the routine, anchor it to the music, watch it back, drill the parts that break, and let your brain rehearse between sessions. Do these and you will learn pieces faster and, just as important, still have them a week later.

step 1Chunk it into eight-counts

Your brain holds a few things at once, not forty. So stop trying to remember the whole routine and start remembering pieces of it. Break the choreography into eight-count chunks and learn one at a time. Get the first eight clean before you touch the second, then link the two, then add the third.

The secret detail most people skip: drill the transitions, not just the chunks. Routines almost always fall apart at the seam between two sections, the moment you have to hand off from one piece to the next. Once two chunks are solid on their own, practice the join between them as its own little chunk. That is where the forgetting lives.

Learn the pieces, then learn the seams between them.

step 2Anchor every move to the music

This is the one that changes everything. A move floating in space is hard to recall. A move pinned to a specific sound in the song recalls itself. Counts are the skeleton: knowing a move lands on the 5 tells you exactly when it happens. But go further and tie moves to the actual sounds in the track. The turn happens when the trumpet hits. The freeze lands when the bass drops. The body roll rides the sustained string.

Once you have done that, the music stops being background and becomes a cue track running underneath you. You stop recalling the routine from memory and start letting the song trigger each move. If you are not yet confident hearing those cues, our guide on counting music for dance is the place to start.

A move tied to a sound cues itself. Let the song carry the routine.

step 3Record it and watch it back

Repetition with your body is only half of it. Repetition with your eyes is the other half, and it is badly underused. Capture the choreography on video, whether that is the choreographer's demo or a clip of yourself, and watch it on repeat. Your brain absorbs the shapes and the timing just from seeing them again and again, even when you are resting.

The catch is friction: scrubbing back and forth through a four-minute video to find the eight counts you are stuck on kills the habit fast. The fix is to attach the video to the exact section you are drilling, so the right few seconds are one tap away and loop on their own.

Watch the hard eight-count on repeat, not the whole video once.

step 4Loop and slow the parts that break

Every routine has one or two spots that fight you. Do not keep running the whole piece to take another swing at them, that is mostly wasted time. Isolate the trouble section, loop it, and slow it down until you can do it cleanly, then bring the speed back up. Targeted reps on the exact problem will fix it far faster than another full run-through. Our guides on looping and slowing a song down go deep on the technique.

Drill the one hard part, not the whole routine again.

step 5Rehearse between sessions

The learning does not stop when the music does. Two old tricks make a real difference. Mark it: run the routine at half energy, just tracing the shapes and the order, to rehearse the sequence without tiring yourself out. Run it in your head: away from the floor, on the train or in line for coffee, mentally walk through the piece and imagine the music. Wherever you get stuck in your head is exactly the spot to clean up next time you practice.

And spread it out. Three short sessions across three days will plant a routine far more durably than one long cram, because your brain files repeated, spaced information as something worth keeping.

Short and often beats long and once. Let your brain rehearse off the floor.

Frequently asked questions

How can I memorize choreography faster?

Break the routine into eight-count chunks and master one at a time, tie each move to a specific musical cue, record it and watch it back, and loop and slow down the hard parts. Short, frequent practice sessions beat one long cram.

Why do I forget choreography so quickly?

Usually because you are trying to memorize the whole routine as a flat list of disconnected steps, which is the hardest possible way to remember it. Chunking the routine, anchoring each move to the music, and drilling the transitions between chunks makes it stick.

What is the fastest way to learn a dance routine?

Chunk the routine into eight-counts, attach each move to the music so the song cues you, record it and rewatch the hard sections, loop and slow the parts that break, and run it in your head between sessions. Structure and the music do most of the work.

Does recording yourself help you learn choreography?

Yes. Watching the choreographer's clip or a recording of yourself on repeat is one of the most effective memory tools, especially when each section is marked so you can jump straight to the part you are drilling instead of scrubbing through the whole video.

How long does it take to memorize a dance routine?

It varies with your experience and the complexity of the piece. But chunked, spaced practice with the music as your guide is consistently faster than repeating the whole song over and over, because your reps land where the routine actually breaks.
